## Abstract

Metal fluoride thin films are important materials in a multitude of applications. Currently, they are mostly used in optics, but their potential in energy harvesting and storage is recognized as well. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) is an advanced thin film deposition method that has an ever-increasing role in microelectronics. The assets of ALD are its capability to produce uniform, stoichiometric, and pure films with precise thickness control even on top of complicated structures, such as high aspect ratio trenches. These characteristics can be beneficial in applications of metal fluoride thin films but so far ALD of metal fluorides has remained much less studied and used than ALD of metal oxides, nitrides, sulfides, and pure metals. This review aims to motivate research on ALD of metal fluorides by surveying potential applications for ALD metal fluoride thin films and coatings. The basics of luminescent applications, antireflection coatings, and lithium-ion batteries will be discussed. Next, the fundamentals of ALD will be presented followed by a comprehensive summary of the metal fluoride ALD processes published so far.

## 1 Introduction

Atomic layer deposition (ALD) is a sophisticated thin film deposition method that produces stoichiometric, and uniform conformal films with extremely good thickness control, also on complicated structures.[1] Due to the versatility of the method, it is used in many applications such as solar cells, protective coatings, and optical applications.[2-4] However, ALD is best known for its role in semiconductor technology: the adoption of ALD enabled the semiconductor industry to take a huge leap in the first decade of 2000′s.[5] It is often stated that ALD is and will be the enabler of keeping up with Moore's law by enabling the manufacturing of smaller and smaller components.

Since the turn of the millennium, the number of materials deposited with ALD has grown fast. Today, ALD processes cover many simple but important semiconductor industry materials such as metal oxides, sulfides and nitrides, and metals.[6] ALD research has reached a stage where most developments are made in the deposition of quaternary materials and electropositive metals as well as fine-tuning the existing processes to meet the requirements of industrial applications. Despite the success in ALD of the advanced materials, there are some groups of simple materials that are lagging behind in basic research. One of these groups is metal fluorides.

The lack of ALD processes for metal fluorides is related to the lack of suitable fluoride precursors, but also to the lack of applications in semiconductor industry, which has been the driving force for ALD process development. From the fluoride precursor perspective, the main challenge is to find a good replacement for the efficient but toxic hydrogen fluoride. Often, the use of milder ALD precursors has come at the expense of the purity of the films.

While metal fluorides lack applications in the semiconductor industry, the potential of metal fluoride thin films has been recognized in, e.g., lithium-ion and sodium-ion batteries and antireflection coatings.[7-9] Transition metal fluorides such as FeF2 and CoF2 are potential materials for conversion cathodes in lithium-ion and/or sodium-ion batteries thanks to their high reaction potentials. LiF and AlF3 have in turn been studied for artificial interphase layers in lithium-ion batteries.[10, 11]

In antireflection coatings mainly alkaline earth and rare earth fluorides are of interest. In fact, e.g. MgF2, LaF3 and GdF3 are among the few materials that fulfill the requirements for antireflection coatings in deep UV lithography.

With rare earth fluorides, especially, the use of ALD is not limited to planar thin films. In fact, rare earth fluorides have interesting applications in nanoparticle coatings, e.g., in medical applications[12] and ALD could provide good-quality thin film coatings for these applications. In addition to conventional thin films and nanoparticle coatings, ALD has potential also in applications where uniform doping of ions into a film is needed. Luminescent devices are an excellent example of this. These devices are one of the most important applications for rare earth fluorides.

The aim of this review is to motivate research on ALD of metal fluorides by giving an overview of the potential importance of these processes. We will start with a survey of current and prospective applications of metal fluoride thin films and coatings on macroscopic substrates and nanoparticles as well as dopants. The principle of ALD is then presented to give a background to readers not yet familiar with the method. All the existing ALD metal fluoride processes are comprehensively listed and described through selected examples. The conclusions section discusses the challenges and future perspectives of ALD metal fluoride research.

## 2 Optical Applications

### 2.1 Antireflection Coatings

There are numerous devices where the reflection of light needs to be suppressed. Antireflection coatings (ARCs) are needed for example in cameras, microscopes, solar cells, telescopes, and photolithography. ARCs are used to suppress annoying reflections, improve contrast, and increase transmission. In this chapter, the basic principles of minimizing reflection and absorption are discussed.

**Figure**1 shows a substrate that has been coated with an AR coating. The R vectors denote the light reflected from the surface without entering the film (R1) and the light reflected from the AR coating/substrate interface (R2). The role of an AR coating is to make the two reflections cancel each other. Both the physical thickness of the AR coating and the refractive indices of the materials have to be considered.[13]

In the case of light coming from a medium with a lower refractive index to a medium with a higher refractive index, the phase of the reflected light changes by 180°. In Equation 1, na denotes the refractive index of air, ns of the substrate, and nf of the coating. If the refractive indices are in the order na<nf<ns, there is a 180° phase change both when the light reflects from the air/film interface and the film-substrate interface. To cancel out the reflections, the path length difference of the light has to be λ/2 (or its odd multiple: 3λ/2, 5λ/2 etc.) for normal incidence. However, the change in the refractive index causes a change in the wavelength of the light: in a film with the refractive index nf, the wavelength is λ/nf. The physical thickness of the AR coating needed to obtain the desired phase change is obtained most conveniently by considering the optical thickness (nfdf) of the film that must equal to λ/4, because the ray reflected from the film-substrate interface passes the film twice. This gives a physical thickness as shown in Equation 2.[13]

For air, the refractive index is 1.00. Hence, in the case of glass (ns = 1.52) the film material should have a refractive index of 1.23 for the reflectance to be zero. In practice, the lowest achievable refractive index for a dense coating is 1.38 of magnesium fluoride, and MgF2 is thus commonly used. A λ/4 MgF2 coating is able to reduce the reflection of light on glass from 4% without the coating to < 1.3%. It should be noted that the refractive index of a material can be lowered by making the coating less dense, i.e., by introducing pores in the coating.[14]

Note that the λ/4 optical thickness can be achieved only with one wavelength. Similarly, the thickness is optimized only for one incident angle of light. At other wavelengths or incidence angles, the total path traveled by the light is different, and thus the reflections do not completely cancel out each other. When antireflection properties are needed over a wide wavelength range or wide angles of incidence light, special multilayer coatings have to be designed.[15]

When very low reflectance at one wavelength is needed, a stack of low and higher refractive index material layers of λ/4 optical thickness are used. The optimal ratio of the refractive indices of the materials is dictated by Equation 3, when light propagates from air to the material with a low refractive index (nL) and then to the material with a higher refractive index (nH). For example, in the case of a glass substrate and MgF2 as the low-index material, the refractive index of the other material should be 1.70.[15]**Figure**2 illustrates the refractive indices of some metal fluoride and oxide thin films at different wavelength ranges.

When eliminating reflections with antireflection coatings, usually it is essential also to avoid absorption of light in the coating. Transparent antireflection coatings are needed for example in lenses in photolithography, cameras, and eyeglasses. If the AR coatings are to be used at the UV wavelengths, the absorbance of, e.g., oxides becomes remarkable. Contrary to oxides, alkali metal fluorides, alkaline earth metal fluorides, and rare earth fluorides have high band gaps and are thus suitable AR coatings also for the UV wavelengths. In practice, alkaline earth metal fluorides and rare earth fluorides are more suitable than alkali metal fluorides due to their better stability in atmospheric conditions.

In addition to reflection and absorption, also scattering of light affects the antireflection properties of the coating. In principle, the smoother the coating is, the less scattering of light is occurring. The AR coating manufacturing method has thus a major role in eliminating the scattering losses.

Other general demands for the AR deposition method are the ability to produce pure films to decrease the amount of light absorbing impurities. The film thickness controllability should be extremely good. Also, coating non-planar objects such as lenses uniformly and conformally should be possible. Traditionally, physical vapor deposition (PVD), chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and sol-gel methods have been popular in antireflection coating deposition, but given the unique properties of ALD, such as excellent thickness control and uniformity, it is likely to become more popular in coming years.

### 2.2 luminescence

Among metal fluorides, rare earth fluorides have the special property of being efficient luminescent materials. The applicability of rare earth ions, or more precisely lanthanides, as colorful light-producing systems is based on their extraordinary electronic structure. In the periodic table, lanthanides are the elements in which the 4f orbitals start to be occupied. For example, in elemental terbium the electron configuration is [Xe] 4f9 5d0 6s2, and in the terbium 3+ ion it is [Xe] 4f8 5d0 6s0. The 4f electrons of lanthanides are shielded by the 5s and 5p electrons and consequently only weakly coupled with phonons.[19] This results in discrete energy levels and sharp 4f-4f transitions. In many lanthanide ions, the most interesting 4f-4f transitions occur at visible wavelengths, e.g., green for Tb3+, red for Eu3+, and blue for Tm3+. Due to the shielding by the outer electrons, the effect of the chemical environment on the energy of 4f levels is also very weak. Thus, the energies of the 4f-4f transitions – and therefore the color of the emitted light – are similar in different kinds of chemical environments.

Because the 4f-4f transitions are forbidden by the Laporte rules, the absorption coefficients for these transitions are low.[19] Therefore, the luminescent centers usually need to be accompanied by sensitizer ions. An example of this is the Ce3+/Tb3+ pair. In this system, UV light is first absorbed by the Laporte-allowed 4f-5d transitions in the Ce3+ ions, and the energy is then transferred non-radiatively to Tb3+. The characteristic green light emission occurs when the terbium ions relax radiatively (**Figure**4).

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/337a33ad-c175-43f3-8351-8ab716c4c5f7/admi1398-fig-0004-m.png)](/cms/asset/b0c84cd3-b617-44a7-971b-678499a27589/admi1398-fig-0004-m.jpg)

The transfer of energy between rare earth ions is nicely demonstrated also in upconversion and downconversion processes. In an anti-Stokes process called upconversion, two or more photons of low energy, e.g., near-infrared (NIR), are converted to one photon of higher energy, e.g., visible light. The upconversion process can occur in systems where only one type of active ion is present but also with the help of sensitizer ions. For the upconversion to occur, there must be a long-lived, metastable intermediate state available. The most common mechanisms[21] are presented here:

1. In a one-ion system, the low-energy photon is first absorbed by the active ion that is excited to the intermediate state. Subsequently, another photon is absorbed, and the ion is excited from the intermediate state to an even higher energy level. The overall outcome is that the energy finally emitted by the ion is higher than the energy of the originally absorbed photons. This mechanism is known by the name excited states absorption (ESA). The ESA mechanism is schematically presented in **Figure****5**a.
2. In the sensitizer-activator process, two (or more) sensitizer ions absorb one photon each and excite to higher energy levels. One sensitizer ion then transfers the energy non-radiatively to the active ion so that the active ion is excited. This is followed by a second non-radiative energy transfer from another sensitizer ion to the active ion, and the active ion is excited to a higher energy level. This process is called energy transfer upconversion (ETU). The ETU mechanism is schematically presented in Figure 5b.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/a469b61f-9621-4eaa-883e-b5eede2e2226/admi1398-fig-0005-m.png)](/cms/asset/1f32759d-35e1-4c8b-8bfb-c311c5c8e1b1/admi1398-fig-0005-m.jpg)

In the downconversion, the absorption of a high-energy photon eventually results in the emission of two or more low-energy photons. There are several ways how the downconversion can occur,[22] e.g.:

1. An excited sensitizer ion transfers some of its energy to the activator, which then relaxes producing (usually) visible light. The remaining energy in the sensitizer can then be transferred to a second activator ion, followed by visible light emission.
2. The sensitizer first transfers energy to the activator as in the case 1) but then emits itself the remaining energy instead of transferring it to a second activator ion.
3. The sensitizer ion first emits a visible photon, and then transfers the remaining energy to the activator.

A drawback of the energy transfer between lanthanides is luminescence quenching. If the concentration of the luminescent centers is too high, the probability of nonradiative relaxation between neighboring lanthanides increases. Therefore, the luminescent centers are always embedded in a host structure to ensure sufficient distance between them.[23]

Although the chemical environment has a very weak effect on the individual emission lines, the host structure should be chosen carefully. The main reason is again related to de-excitation. If the host structure has high phonon energies (lattice vibration energies), the de-excitation might occur non-radiatively through coupling with the host lattice vibrations. Good host structures are for example metal fluorides which have in general low phonon energies and high thermal and chemical stability. In addition to the low phonon energies, the cation of the host fluoride should preferably be of the same size as the active ion, such as in NaYF4, to reduce lattice defects and stress.[23] In practice, the extent of the luminescence quenching is also determined by for example the purity, crystal phase, and morphology of the host material.[23]

In addition to light as discussed above, luminescence can be excited by the impact of, e.g., electrons, ionizing radiation, friction, or pressure on the material. The most convenient and widespread ways to excite the rare earth ions are electrons and photons. For electroluminescence, perhaps the most well-known application is electroluminescent displays, where also TbF3 embedded in ZnS has been studied as a luminescent center.[24] However, as fluorides are intrinsically poor electronic conductors and thus not, in general, suitable for electroluminescence, those applications are not covered in this review. Rather, this work concentrates on photoluminescence applications. Examples of possible applications are presented in the following subchapters.

#### 2.2.1 Anticounterfeiting

Combinations of different emission lines of the lanthanides can be utilized in security applications, such as in bank notes and tags for documentation. The idea is to create a complex optical fingerprint consisting of several rare earth emission lines. Upon illumination, the object under investigation emits its characteristic luminescence pattern. The emission is then compared to the emission pattern of the known reference.

The operation principle of anticounterfeiting tags can be divided into two groups according to the complexity of the security mechanism.[25]

1. single-level luminescence
2. multi-level luminescence

In single-level luminescence, the security tag is inspected in one step. For example, the tag under inspection is illuminated at one wavelength and, after an upconversion process, several emission lines are seen. The exact color of the emission is dependent on the concentration of the luminescent centers. Creating new tags is relatively straightforward. However, forging the patterns is not overly complicated either.

Multi-level luminescence utilizes many inspection steps. These can involve illumination by different wavelengths or illumination power or exposure to elevated temperatures etcetera. Luminescence lifetime may also be exploited. It is clear that mimicking the complicated luminescence pattern is troublesome, but so is the designing of it.

Even though the combinations used in real applications are not disclosed, the outcome of illuminating lanthanide-doped NaREF4 microrod/core-shell particle hybrids was nicely presented in the article by Hu et al.[26] A change in the emission color was obtained by a temperature increase (**Figure**6). In the work by Zhao et al., NaYF4:Yb3+, Er3+ was made in thin film form by electrodeposition, and the possibility of using it in anticounterfeiting applications was suggested.[27] Dong et al., in turn, made flexible anticounterfeiting labels by preparing NaGdF4:Yb3+, Er3+@carbon dot nanoparticles by a solvothermal method and depositing those on cellulose films.[28]

#### 2.2.2 Solid State Lighting

Solid state lighting is an emerging application for rare earth photoluminescence. The motivation stems from the need for energy efficient and high-quality sun-light mimicking light for, e.g., display backlights and lighting in general.

In a solid state, white light can be generated by combining the emission of three LEDs: red, green, and blue (**Figure****7**a). The drawback of this approach is however that it is intolerant to changes in individual LEDs. Alternatively, white light can be generated by using a blue LED and a yellow phosphor (Figure 7b), but this approach produces cold white light. A third option is to use blue (Figure 7c) or UV LED (Figure 7d) in combination with phosphors that emit red, green, (and blue) light. Rare earth ions embedded in a fluoride matrix can offer bright emission of different colors for this purpose. In addition, if needed, the tone of the white light can be tuned by adjusting the rare earth dopant concentrations.[29, 30]

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/82a5403e-7c51-4d77-b4c4-783f776c2b2d/admi1398-fig-0007-m.png)](/cms/asset/dcf9cc28-8d79-49be-ab8c-2d7efd8613c9/admi1398-fig-0007-m.jpg)

Sayed et al. produced white light by Eu3+, Tb3+, and Dy3+ doped CeF3 nanoparticles.[31] The best results were obtained with the dopant contents of 2.0 mol% of Tb3+(green), 3.5 mol% of Dy3+ (blue), and 4.5 mol% of Eu3+ (red). When the material was illuminated with a UV lamp, daylight-like emission was obtained. To mimic the structure of a solid-state lighting device, a transparent nanocomposite was made by embedding the CeF3:Tb3+, Dy3+, Eu3+ nanoparticles in PMMA. Bright emission upon UV illumination was observed also in this case.

It is possible to create a white light-emitting phosphor system also by doping one kind of rare earth ion with many emission lines into a host structure (**Figure**8). Li et al. made Eu3+ doped GdF3 nanofibers and illuminated these with UV light.[32] By adjusting the Eu3+ content, light emission from blue to white and red was observed. The color changes are due to the different concentration quenching behavior of the emission lines.

#### 2.2.3 Bioimaging

Rare earth fluoride-based nanoparticles have been studied as substituents for organic dyes and quantum dots in bioimaging. Their benefits are photostability, chemical robustness, long luminescence lifetime, and low toxicity.[33, 34] For the bioimaging purposes, the rare earth fluoride nanoparticles can be attached to organic molecules that are tailored to bind to specific targets, e.g., tumors. These particles are delivered into tissues and once they have reached the target, they are illuminated. The resulting luminescence is recorded, giving information about the tumor (**Figure**9).

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/68ceb9ad-023f-4980-81fe-5d6f38874e4a/admi1398-fig-0009-m.png)](/cms/asset/24f7466c-d266-4a30-b65b-f7a5520e799e/admi1398-fig-0009-m.jpg)

Rare earth fluoride nanoparticles such as NaGdF4 that upconvert NIR light to visible light have gained a lot of attention. Biological tissues absorb the least in the NIR range, and therefore NIR light can penetrate deeper in the tissues than UV and visible light. In addition, using NIR light for the excitation gives rise to less autofluorescence and hence enhanced image contrast.[34] Especially interesting research was done by Vetrone et al. who coated the upconverting NaGdF4:Er3+, Yb3+ nanoparticles with a NaGdF4:Yb3+layer.[35] As compared to the uncoated particles, the luminescence yield was improved because the nonradiative quenching on the surface defects was suppressed. Also, the Yb3+ ions in the NaGdF4:Yb3+ layer served as additional sensitizers. The coating of the nanoparticles was done by solution methods, but the study is inspiring also from the ALD point of view.

Compared to other rare earth fluorides, gadolinium-based host structures have additional advantages due to their magnetic properties. Gd3+ containing bioimaging host materials can simultaneously serve as contrast agents for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Gd3+ has seven unpaired electrons, a large magnetic moment, and a long electronic relaxation time. It thus efficiently reduces proton relaxation times in water molecules, improving the contrast between healthy and diseased tissues.[36, 37]

## 3 Negative Thermal Expansion

A unique member of the rare earth fluorides is scandium fluoride. Instead of optical applications, the main interest is in its extraordinary thermal behavior. Scandium fluoride is one of the rare examples of materials that possess a negative thermal expansion coefficient, i.e., it contracts upon heating. In addition, the contraction is observed over a wide temperature range, from ≈10 to 1100 K. Research on both the fundamental properties of ScF3 as well as tuning of the thermal properties has been active ever since the negative thermal expansion of ScF3 was experimentally proved by Greve et al. in 2010.[39]

The cubic phase of ScF3 consists of corner-sharing [ScF6] octahedra, which form a ReO3-type crystal structure, similar to many other negative thermal expansion materials (**Figure****10**a). By using powder x-ray diffraction (XRD), Greve et al. verified that the negative thermal expansion is encountered also in ScF3.[39] They suggested the behavior results from transverse vibrations of the fluoride ions and the rocking motion of the [ScF6] octahedra. Later, thermal properties of ScF3 have been studied with, e.g., neutron scattering, diffuse x-ray scattering, and extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS).[40-42] It was verified that the contraction of the structure upon heating is indeed a result of the tilt of the [ScF6] octahedra simultaneously with the distortion of Sc-F bonds. The thermally induced vibrations of the fluoride ions occur mainly perpendicular to the Sc–F–Sc bonds, resulting in a guitar string-like motion. The scandium ions are pulled closer to the central fluoride and hence closer to each other. Simultaneously, the octahedra tilt toward the center void as illustrated in Figure 10b.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/c979e92a-3c60-4815-9038-a22d1d097bad/admi1398-fig-0010-m.png)](/cms/asset/e79b5230-3b04-4341-a2d8-08c280f987ea/admi1398-fig-0010-m.jpg)

Besides the cubic ScF3, the hexagonal form of ScF3 shows negative thermal expansion.[44] Hexagonal ScF3 consists of [ScF6] octahedra as well, but they form hexagonal rings with voids in the middle (**Figure**11). Also in the hexagonal phase, the tilt of the octahedra has been observed upon annealing. However, as opposed to the cubic form, the slight distortion is preserved in the structure after the material cools down.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/a4219ca4-2bfa-471f-a658-78d8ed486527/admi1398-fig-0011-m.png)](/cms/asset/4e1188e0-724d-4b17-b155-fed52b496071/admi1398-fig-0011-m.jpg)

In devices operating at elevated temperatures, different thermal expansions of materials induce stress. Deformation caused by stress can have detrimental effects on the performance of the devices, especially in high-precision applications such as optical mirrors.[45] In the semiconductor industry, an important challenge is the thermal mismatch of silicon and other components such as heat sinks.[45] Extreme-case examples of thermal compatibility challenges are solid oxide fuel cells (SOFC) which operate at temperatures as high as 1000 °C, and demand compatibility of the thermal properties of electrodes, electrolytes, and interconnects.[46]

Negative thermal expansion materials offer a way to counteract the common expansion. By combining them with positive thermal expansion materials, the expansion can be suppressed, and even zero thermal expansion (ZTE) materials can be obtained. Several approaches have been taken to modify also the thermal expansion coefficient of ScF3. Doping of ScF3 with at least Y3+, Ti3+, Al3+, Fe3+, Zr4+, and Fe3+ + Ga3+ has been studied.[43, 47-51] The replacement of scandium ions with other cations leads to distortions of the structure and therefore changes in the thermal behavior. With a careful choice of the doping ratio, close to zero thermal expansion coefficients have been obtained. As an example, Morelock et al. synthesized Sc1-xAlxF3 by a solid-state reaction of ScF3 and AlF3 powders and obtained close to zero thermal expansion at the temperature range of ∼600 to 800 K with x = 0.15.[49]

Another approach for creating a ZTE material is to construct a composite structure from ScF3 and a positive thermal expansion material. Xiao et al. made a ScF3@Cu core-shell structure by plating copper on ScF3 particles.[52] They reported thermal expansion and thermal conductivity as a function of the copper content (**Figure**12). With a 40% Cu volume fraction (ScF3@Cu-40), a coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) close to that of silicon was obtained. Importantly, the thermal conductivity of the core-shell structures is also good, making these structures interesting heat sink materials for integrated circuits.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/6531d8a4-391d-4393-8b3b-f6c86bec647a/admi1398-fig-0012-m.png)](/cms/asset/9fe4f2d9-465c-4fe7-b9c7-292a65a56946/admi1398-fig-0012-m.jpg)

It remains to be seen how thin film deposition methods such as ALD will contribute to the development of negative/zero thermal expansion coefficient materials. The first report on ScF3 deposition by ALD was recently published, which opens doors for further materials property optimization.[53]

## 4 Energy Applications

### 4.1 Solar Cells

Rare earth fluorides have multiple applications in solar cells. Due to their unique luminescent properties, they can serve as upconversion or downconversion materials. Rare earth fluorides can also be used as electron-selective contacts.

In general, no matter which solar cell technique is in question, one of the limiting factors is that only a part of the Sun's spectrum can be efficiently converted to energy. Photons that have lower energy than the semiconductor bandgap are not absorbed. In turn, photons having higher energy than the band gap form hole–electron pairs, but the excess photon energy is lost as heat. Rare earth upconversion and downconversion can be used to improve the efficiency of solar cells. Studies on the use of rare earth fluorides in at least amorphous silicon solar cells (a-Si), dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs), and organic solar cells (OSC) have been published.[54]

As an example of an upconversion system, Cheng et al. synthesized NaGdF4:Yb3+, Er3+ microcrystals for a-Si solar cells[55] where the high wavelength limit for the absorption of light is 708 nm.[54] Upon illuminating the NaGdF4:Yb3+, Er3+ microcrystals with 980 nm light, the energy is first absorbed by the Yb3+ ions and then transferred to the Er3+ ions. The wavelength of the light finally emitted by the Er3+ ions is in the range of ≈435–654 nm and can thus be absorbed by the a-Si solar cell. In the solar cell structure, the NaGdF4:Yb3+, Er3+ microcrystals were embedded in polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA). The best results were obtained when the upconversion layer was placed on the rear side of the solar cell and combined with a back reflector (**Figure**13). With this configuration, the number of photons emitted toward the solar cell was maximized, and a 10-fold enhancement in the photocurrent compared to a solar cell without the UC layer was measured. A downconversion system can be constructed from the same host, NaGdF4, with, e.g., Eu3+ as the active ion.[56] Gd3+ ions act as sensitizers for the active ion. Naturally, in the case of the downconversion, the layer must be placed in front of the absorber.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/396a79c1-f87c-4fda-8230-70a31a6ee1ad/admi1398-fig-0013-m.png)](/cms/asset/e1ce29fc-79e7-4a82-a19d-7085d21e1402/admi1398-fig-0013-m.jpg)

Rare earth fluorides have recently found new applications as electron-selective contacts in crystalline silicon solar cells (c-Si). Passivating contacts are needed between the c-Si and the metal electrodes to suppress the recombination of electrons and holes. Usually, a thin Si layer doped with phosphorus has been used on the collector. However, doping with phosphorus adds complexity and hazards in the manufacturing process, and also parasitic absorption of light is an issue.[57] Rare earth fluorides as contacts could provide simple manufacturing, less parasitic absorption, and good electron selectivity. At least YF3, CeF3, EuFx, and GdF3 have been reported as possible contact materials.[58-61] For example, in the work by Chen et al., GdF3 films were deposited by thermal evaporation between the n-Si and Al in a partial rear-contact c-Si solar cell (**Figure****14**a).[61] The 1.4 to 5.6 nm thick GdF3 films improved the power conversion efficiency from 17.40 to 20.71% as compared to the n-Si/Al structure without the GdF3 layer (Figure 14b).

### 4.2 Batteries

One of the most studied applications for transition metal fluorides is lithium-ion and sodium-ion batteries. Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are considered the best energy storage system for example in transportation due to their excellent energy density and long cycle life.[62] An analogous system to the lithium-ion battery is the sodium-ion battery. Interest toward sodium-based energy storage has risen especially due to the abundance of sodium.[63]

Lithium-ion batteries consist of a cathode, an anode, an electronically insulating electrolyte, a separator, and current collectors (**Figure**15). When the battery is fully charged, lithium ions reside on the anode. Once no voltage is applied, lithium ions move from the anode to the cathode through the electrolyte. Energy is obtained from the system when electrons move from the anode to the cathode through an external circuit to balance the charge inequilibrium caused by the movement of lithium ions (Figure 15). This reaction is spontaneous and driven by an intercalation reaction of the cathode. Once the maximum amount of Li+ ions are occupying the cathode sites, the battery is out of charge. To charge the battery again, the movement of Li+ ions has to be reversed, i.e., a potential has to be applied to the cell. When no potential is applied, and the cell is connected to an external device, spontaneous Li+ ion movement follows once again.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/301b6663-f7c3-4d81-8e2e-ec43fa13b31c/admi1398-fig-0015-m.png)](/cms/asset/7d324d31-e32c-489b-a338-7103c01a2fcf/admi1398-fig-0015-m.jpg)

Typical cathode materials in LIBs are LCO (lithium cobalt oxide LiCoO2) and other layered oxides such as lithium nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC) and lithium iron phosphate (LFP). Graphite is widely used as the anode. The electrolyte is most often a liquid, with LiPF6 dissolved in organic solvent. Solid electrolytes are extensively studied as well, and these can include solid polymer electrolytes or inorganic solid electrolytes such as lithium phosphorus oxynitride (LiPON). The current collectors are made of copper on the anode side and aluminum on the cathode side.

Metal fluorides are attractive materials for various uses in lithium-ion batteries. They can be used as conversion cathode materials, where the multiple electron reduction/oxidation reactions provide a higher capacity than is available with the current state-of-the-art layered oxide cathodes. Another important application is coating electrode materials with thin metal fluoride layers to mitigate typical degradation pathways in the battery.[64]

#### 4.2.1 Conversion Cathodes

The energy densities of current batteries are mostly limited by the cathodes, and therefore cathode research is very active. In addition to the high energy density, other requirements for the cathode material are high operating voltage, fast and reversible reactions with lithium ions, good ionic conductivity and reasonable electronic conductivity, stability with respect to cycling, and reasonably low volume change upon lithiation. In order to increase the energy density of batteries, research has lately focused on conversion cathodes.[9] Transition metal fluorides, especially, have been targeted thanks to their higher reaction potentials compared to metal oxide and metal sulfide conversion cathodes.

Whereas in the intercalation cathodes lithium ions are inserted in the layered structure, with the conversion cathodes the whole operation principle is relying on chemical transformation. During the conversion, lithium ions bond to the anion, in this case fluoride, forming LiF. Simultaneously, the metal ions are reduced to the metallic state and form nanodomains in the insulating LiF matrix. Equation 4 and **Figure**16 describe the process.

#### 4.2.2 Electrode Coatings

The electrodes in traditional lithium-ion batteries suffer from multiple different degradation pathways during extensive cycling[64] (**Figure**18). On the anode side, these include the formation of an unstable solid-electrolyte interphase layer (SEI-layer), particle cracking and lithium dendrite formation. The SEI is especially critical: an unstable SEI layer leads to continuous electrolyte decomposition on the anode side, resulting in a loss of mobile lithium ions and increasing the internal resistance of the battery. On the cathode side, processes like particle cracking and electrolyte decomposition can also play a role. In addition, transition metal dissolution is a major concern. Losing the redox-active transition metal into the electrolyte solution drastically affects the capacity of the battery.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/abdd25fc-b8b6-4de8-ad99-078fe7ee68ae/admi1398-fig-0018-m.png)](/cms/asset/b24ebac1-5d84-4c01-a2ab-f0c5b41fbd22/admi1398-fig-0018-m.jpg)

To mitigate all of these degradation pathways, thin material layers can be deposited onto the electrode materials, either when still in powder form[10] or on the already formed electrode itself[76, 77] (**Figure****19**a). Gas phase methods, dry coating methods such as ball-milling, and wet chemical methods can be used to deposit the coatings.[64, 78] While coating powders is easier to implement into the electrode fabrication process, it can lead to a loss of conducting pathways between the electrode particles and conductive carbon. Therefore, in recent studies, the coating of ready-made electrodes has been preferred. When considering the conditions inside the battery, metal fluorides are attractive materials for electrode coatings. Any coating used on the electrode needs to be electrochemically and chemically stable in the battery conditions. Metal fluorides generally fulfill this requirement well (Figure 19b). In fact, metal fluorides often form spontaneously as part of the anode SEI layer, because the electrolyte contains LiPF6 as the lithium salt. Motivated by the stability of metal fluorides, a range of studies have been conducted on protecting electrode materials with metal fluoride coatings.

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/79c3aed8-27af-45d6-b9a8-49cb1f0633a0/admi1398-fig-0019-m.png)](/cms/asset/e86415e3-1009-4eeb-ac89-e4b5b7849e78/admi1398-fig-0019-m.jpg)

Many metal fluorides have been used for electrode coating, as discussed for example by Kraytsberg et al. in 2015,[10] and Kaur and Gates in 2022[11]. These include main group metal, transition metal, and rare earth metal fluorides, deposited with a variety of methods. In particular, materials such as LiF, AlF3, and mixtures of AlF3 with other fluorides have been popular.[76, 77, 80-84] A lot of emphasis has been put on coating cathode materials because cathodes can be considered to limit the capacity and energy density of current batteries. In general, it can be noted that metal fluoride coatings increase the cycling stability and capacity retention in cathodes by limiting side reactions with the electrolyte. For example, coating the nickel-rich NMC811 cathode with a thin LiAlF4 layer improved the capacity retention of the electrode notably when compared to the uncoated electrode (**Figure****20**a). Fluoride coatings on manganese-rich electrodes such as LMNO and a layered-layered spinel electrode (LLS) resulted in less Mn dissolution during cycling, which can increase the high voltage stability of the electrode[85, 86] (Figure 20b). Coating the cathode can also enable higher cut-off voltages, which can result in a larger energy density for the whole battery.[76] Thus, even though often losing some of the initial capacity due to larger impedance during the very first battery cycles, the increased capacity retention and higher voltages provide an improvement to the overall energy performance of the battery (Figure 20c).[76]

[![Details are in the caption following the image](/cms/asset/594b36a4-f32e-493b-9c85-6f5af0a7996d/admi1398-fig-0020-m.png)](/cms/asset/86a720cd-4a4b-4113-9e52-aa2c16141be6/admi1398-fig-0020-m.jpg)

Artificial SEI layers on anodes have been studied as well. For lithium metal anodes, for example, LiF and AlF3 coatings have been reported to mitigate side reactions and SEI growth and provide more uniform lithium plating and stripping.[82, 83] This can decrease dendrite formation, which in turn leads to a longer cycle life in lithium metal-containing batteries. Improved capacity retention is also seen with graphite anodes coated with AlF3.[87] Similarly, for silicon anodes, a LiF coating has been reported to mitigate electrolyte decomposition on the anode.[81] The alloying reaction between silicon and lithium leads to a large volume expansion, and therefore a hard and brittle fluoride coating might not be the best choice for these anodes on its own; a more flexible coating is most likely required to improve the performance of silicon anodes even further.[88]

When considering metal fluorides as coating materials for electrodes, it is important to note that many fluorides show rather poor lithium-ion conduction. Therefore, it is important to limit the film thickness to minimize the impedance increase caused by slower kinetics through the coating (**Figure**21). Therefore, films as thin as a nanometer to ≈10 nanometers have been used for electrode protection.[76, 77, 85] Making such thin but uniform and conformal films requires the use of advanced thin film deposition methods, such as ALD.[64] Many of the materials listed here could be deposited with ALD in future batteries.[10, 76, 77, 80, 82, 83, 85, 86]

## 5 Atomic Layer Deposition

### 5.1 Principle

Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD), originally known by the name Atomic Layer Epitaxy (ALE), was developed in Finland by Tuomo Suntola in the 1970′s.[89] A similar invention called Molecular Layering was independently made in The Soviet Union in the 60′s.[90, 91] Suntola's motivation was to create a well-controlled thin film deposition method for flat panel electroluminescent displays.[91] The display technology development had been delayed due to insufficient quality of thin films deposited with the methods of sputtering and evaporation available at that time. Atomic Layer Epitaxy was Suntola's successful solution to these issues.

The first material ever deposited by ALD was zinc sulfide which was then doped with manganese to be used as a luminescent layer in the displays. Although ALD had many outstanding properties, it did not yet become widely adopted, mainly due to its slowness. For many years, the applications of ALD were limited mostly to electroluminescent displays.[91]

The real boom of ALD started in the late 90′s when the semiconductor industry became interested in the technique. A deposition method was needed to realize the scale down of components of integrated circuits and thereby keep up with Moore's law. The final breakthrough of ALD came with the hafnium oxide films used as gate oxides.[5] Ever since the 90′s, the variety of applications of ALD has grown fast, now covering fields from, e.g., energy technologies to corrosion protection and medical technology. Despite the slow start, ALD is today considered as the most sophisticated and versatile thin film deposition method in many fields.

ALD is a variation of the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method. The main difference between traditional CVD and ALD is the precursor delivery. Whereas in CVD the precursors are fed into the reactor simultaneously and continuously and react in the gas phase or on the surface at high temperatures, ALD is based on sequential exposure of the substrate surface to the pulses of gaseous precursors (**Figure****22**a).
